Knowing What to Sell, When, and to Whom

V. Kumar, Rajkumar Venkatesan & Werner Reinartz  |  University of Connecticut & INSEAD
▾
CRM AnalyticsBayesian EstimationPurchase Prediction Customer Probability CubeMarketing ROIOvercommunication Effect
Overview & Central Problem ▾

This article addresses one of the most persistent and costly failures in CRM: despite enormous investments in data infrastructure, most companies remain remarkably bad at predicting what a specific customer will buy, which product they will choose, and — crucially — when they will make that purchase. Traditional CRM-based prediction methods achieve accuracy of only about 60%, barely better than a coin toss. Companies routinely send the wrong offer, to the wrong customer, at the wrong time — wasting resources and actively alienating customers through over-communication.

The authors argue the root cause is not a failure of CRM data per se, but a fundamental flaw in the mathematical methods used to analyse it. They propose a superior approach derived from Nobel Prize-winning econometric work and demonstrate that it lifts prediction accuracy to approximately 80%, with direct bottom-line impact in the hundreds of millions of dollars.

"The secret to achieving a good marketing ROI is simple: Give customers more of what they truly want and less of what they don't."
The Traditional Method and Its Fatal Flaws ▾

Two-Step Regression Approach

The dominant industry practice splits the prediction problem into two separate, sequentially solved regression models: (1) a Product Choice Model estimating the probability a customer will choose a particular product, and (2) a Purchase Timing Model estimating the probability of a purchase in a given time period. The joint probability is computed by multiplying these two probabilities, producing what the authors call the Customer Probability Cube (axes: Customers × Products × Time Periods).

Flaw 1: Interdependence Ignored

The timing of a purchase is intrinsically linked to the type of product being purchased. Because the traditional method uses two independent regression equations, it completely ignores the interdependence between product choice and purchase timing — so the resulting joint probabilities are systematically wrong.

Flaw 2: Sampling Error (The Core Problem)

All regression analysis assumes that variable weightings estimated from a sample are representative of the entire population — a statistically untenable assumption. The traditional method has no mechanism to account for or mitigate this sampling uncertainty.

The Customer Probability Cube

A three-dimensional matrix mapping each customer (rows) against each product (columns) and each time period (depth). Each cell contains the probability that Customer i will purchase Product j in Quarter t — enabling highly targeted, resource-efficient campaigns.

The Proposed Solution: Bayesian Estimation ▾

The authors build upon the work of Nobel Laureate Daniel McFadden (Economics, 2000) to develop a multivariate likelihood function that simultaneously models product choice and purchase timing as a unified, interdependent process.

Unlike regression analysis, Bayesian estimation: (1) specifies the full range of weightings that could have plausibly generated the observed data, (2) iteratively searches through this range to find the distribution of coefficients most likely to reproduce the actual observed purchasing patterns, and (3) makes predictions that incorporate this uncertainty.

Metaphorically: regression draws the best-fit line through a scatter plot; Bayesian estimation reproduces the actual scatter of dots.

The Overcommunication Finding ▾

One of the most counterintuitive findings: the relationship between marketing contact frequency and purchase behaviour is highly non-linear — an inverted-U. Below a minimum threshold, communication increases sales. Above an upper threshold, customers experience overload and sales actually decline. Both companies in the study were operating above this upper threshold — actively destroying sales through excessive contact.

The new model reduced communication costs 31% (B2B) and 26% (financial services) while simultaneously increasing revenue.

"Communicating too much can harm you as much as communicating too little. Many companies may be actively damaging their customer revenues in attempts to make sure that no opportunity for a sale is missed."
Key Concepts & Frameworks ▾
Customer Probability Cube

3D matrix mapping customer × product × time period probabilities for precision targeting.

Bayesian Estimation

Models uncertainty in parameter estimates using probability distributions rather than single point estimates.

Sampling Error

The primary flaw of regression-based CRM: coefficients estimated on a sample are not reliably representative.

Interpurchase Timing

Time intervals between purchases — the dimension where traditional models perform worst.

Communication Threshold Effect

Non-linear relationship where both under- and over-communication reduce purchase rates.

McFadden's Duration Analysis

Nobel Prize-winning econometric framework for modelling time-to-event data — the foundation of the authors' likelihood function.

Marketing Myopia

Theodore Levitt  |  Professor Emeritus, Harvard Business School
▾
Customer vs. Product OrientationBusiness Definition Industry DeclineSelf-Deceiving Growth Cycle Creative DestructionSelling vs. Marketing
Overview & Central Argument ▾

Published originally in 1960, this is one of the most influential articles in management history. Levitt's audacious claim: there is no such thing as a growth industry — there are only companies organised to create and capitalise on growth opportunities. Industries decline because management fails them — specifically, because managers define their business around the product they make rather than the customer needs they serve.

"The railroads are in trouble today not because that need was filled by others but because it was not filled by the railroads themselves. They let others take customers away from them because they assumed themselves to be in the railroad business rather than in the transportation business."
The Four Conditions of the Self-Deceiving Growth Cycle ▾
  1. Population Myth: Belief that growth is assured by an expanding population — breeds complacency and kills creative imagination.
  2. No Substitute Myth: Belief there is no competitive substitute — makes firms feel invulnerable and blind to disruptive alternatives.
  3. Mass Production Trap: Excessive faith in the advantages of scale — orients the entire organisation inward, away from the market.
  4. R&D Obsession: Preoccupation with technical excellence — firms staffed by engineers become product-oriented by default.
The Self-Deceiving Cycle

Early success → attribute success to the product, not the customer need → assume growth is automatic → concentrate investment on production and R&D → customer orientation atrophies → substitutes emerge from outside → industry declines while convinced of its own health.

The Canonical Examples ▾

The Railroads

Defined themselves as being in the railroad business rather than the transportation business — so they watched automobiles, trucks, and aircraft capture their customers without mounting any serious competitive response.

Hollywood and Television

Established film studios defined themselves as being in the movie business, treating television as a mortal threat rather than a new channel for their true purpose: entertainment. Had they been in the entertainment business, television would have been an expansive new opportunity.

The Petroleum Industry

Defined their business as finding crude oil → refining it → selling gasoline. They never asked: what do motorists actually want? The answer: frictionless personal mobility. They failed repeatedly to capitalise on natural gas, fuel cells, battery vehicles, and solar energy — all being developed by outsiders.

Other Vignettes

  • Dry Cleaning: Disrupted by synthetic fibres that required less cleaning — a substitute from a completely different direction.
  • Grocery Chains: Dismissed supermarkets as "cheapy." Those who held their ground were wiped out.
  • Buggy Whip Manufacturers: No product improvement could save the industry once the automobile displaced the horse.
Selling vs. Marketing & The Henry Ford Paradox ▾
SellingMarketing
Focused on the needs of the sellerFocused on the needs of the buyer

The Henry Ford Paradox: Ford's genius was marketing, not production. He started with the insight that at $500 per car, millions would buy one. Mass production was the result of his marketing thinking. Ford's eventual failure — insisting on only black cars — was a descent into product orientation, and allowed General Motors to surpass him.

"The entire corporation must be viewed as a customer-creating and customer-satisfying organism. Management must think of itself not as producing products but as providing customer-creating value satisfactions."
Key Concepts & Enduring Relevance ▾
Marketing Myopia

Management's inability to see beyond the current product to the broader customer need — creating strategic blind spots.

Business Definition

Narrow definition creates myopia; broad definition around customer need opens growth horizons.

Creative Destruction

Firms must proactively obsolete their own products before outsiders do, even when those products are currently profitable.

Population Myth

The dangerous assumption that demographic growth automatically sustains industry growth.

Customer Primacy

The customer should be the starting point for all business strategy — product is a consequence, not a cause.

Schumpeterian Disruption

Levitt's implicit argument: self-disrupt or be disrupted. The 2004 republication is confirmed by Netflix, Amazon, EVs.

The Elements of Value

Eric Almquist, John Senior & Nicolas Bloch  |  Bain & Company
▾
30 Elements of ValueValue PyramidMaslow's Hierarchy Net Promoter ScoreCustomer LoyaltyRevenue GrowthDiscrete Choice Analysis
Overview & Central Argument ▾

Through three decades of consumer research and a study of 10,000+ U.S. consumers rating nearly 50 companies, the authors identify 30 fundamental "elements of value" — universal building blocks of what consumers want. These elements are organised into a four-level pyramid (modelled on Maslow) and empirically demonstrated to predict customer loyalty (NPS) and revenue growth.

"The right combinations of value elements pay off in stronger customer loyalty, greater consumer willingness to try a particular brand, and sustained revenue growth."
The Elements of Value Pyramid ▾
Social Impact Life Changing Emotional Functional
Higher levels represent more powerful but rarer forms of value

Level 1: Functional (Base)

Saves time · Simplifies · Makes money · Reduces risk · Organises · Integrates · Connects · Reduces effort · Avoids hassles · Reduces cost · Quality · Variety · Sensory appeal · Informs

Level 2: Emotional

Reduces anxiety · Rewards me · Nostalgia · Design/Aesthetics · Badge value · Wellness · Therapeutic value · Fun/Entertainment · Attractiveness · Provides access

Level 3: Life-Changing

Provides hope · Self-actualisation · Motivation · Heirloom · Affiliation & belonging

Level 4: Social Impact (Apex)

Self-transcendence — enables contribution to something larger than oneself (e.g., TOMS Shoes: buy one, give one)

Key Empirical Findings ▾

More Elements = Dramatically Higher Loyalty

Companies scoring high (8+/10) on four or more elements achieved 3× the NPS of companies with only one high score, and 20× the NPS of companies with no high scores. Apple scored highly on 11 of the 30 elements.

More Elements = Faster Revenue Growth

Companies with high scores on four or more elements had revenue growth four times greater than companies with only one high score.

Amazon Prime — Deliberate Value Stacking

  1. 2005 launch: Reduces cost + Saves time (unlimited 2-day shipping for $79/year)
  2. Expansion: Added Provides access + Fun/entertainment (Prime Video)
  3. Further expansion: Added Reduces risk (unlimited photo storage)
  4. Result: 8 elements; ~40% U.S. retail market penetration; price raised to $99/year

The Universal Primacy of Quality

Across all industries, perceived quality is the single most powerful predictor of customer advocacy. It is a necessary but not sufficient condition — the entry ticket.

Digital-native companies dramatically outperform on functional elements (saves time, avoids hassles). Brick-and-mortar retailers retain advantages on emotional and life-changing elements — badge value, attractiveness, affiliation — because human interaction delivers emotional value digital channels cannot replicate.

Key Concepts & Frameworks ▾
Value Stacking

Strategically and sequentially adding new value elements to a core offering over time — the Amazon Prime model.

Maslow's Hierarchy (Applied)

The theoretical architecture: functional needs first; emotional and life-changing elements differentiate once the functional baseline is met.

Net Promoter Score (NPS)

Customer loyalty metric used as the primary outcome variable to validate the model's predictive power.

Laddering Technique

Qualitative interviewing method that probes beneath stated preferences to uncover underlying psychological motivations.

Discrete Choice Analysis

Customers choose between hypothetical product configurations, revealing the relative importance of each attribute.

Jobs-to-Be-Done (Christensen)

Complementary framework — the elements of value provide a structured taxonomy for answering "what job is the customer hiring this product to do?"

The Science of Sensory Marketing

Aradhna Krishna  |  University of Michigan Sensory Marketing Laboratory
▾
Embodied CognitionFive SensesNon-conscious Stimuli Sensory CongruenceBrand PersonalityOlfactory MarketingTactile Perception
Overview & Central Argument ▾

For two decades, marketers in various industries have been building expertise in reaching consumers through the five senses — learning to deploy cues such as the sting from a swig of mouthwash and the scritch-scratch sound of a Sharpie pen, that intensify brand perceptions. The past year (2014–15) brought a rush of academic interest. New research suggests we are entering an era in which many more consumer products companies will exploit sense-based marketing.

At the heart of the new wave of research is embodied cognition — the idea that, without our conscious awareness, our bodily sensations help determine the decisions we make. Sensory influences are subtle — and that is exactly why they are so powerful. Consumers don't perceive them as marketing messages and therefore don't react with the usual resistance to ads and promotions.

"In the past, communications with customers were essentially monologues — companies just talked at consumers. Then they evolved into dialogues. Now they're becoming multidimensional conversations, with products finding their own voices and consumers responding viscerally and subconsciously to them."
— Aradhna Krishna
Key Characters & Researchers ▾
Aradhna Krishna
Director, Sensory Marketing Laboratory, University of Michigan — foremost expert in the field

Author of Customer Sense: How the 5 Senses Influence Buying Behavior (2013). Discovered that senses amplify one another when congruent. Core question: why does cinnamon smell make a heating pad seem to work better?

S. Adam Brasel
Associate Professor of Marketing, Boston College

States that work on embodied cognition has begun "blowing up on the academic side." Reported more papers on sensory research at the 2014 ACR conference than at any previous conference.

Lawrence E. Williams & John A. Bargh
University of Colorado & Yale

Demonstrated embodied cognition: people who briefly held a warm beverage were more likely than those who held a cold one to judge a stranger as friendly.

Xun (Irene) Huang
Sun Yat-sen University

Research showed warm ambient temperatures prompted people to conform to a crowd — choosing the TV remote preferred by most others, matching stock price predictions, betting on the "favourite" in a horse race.

Chuck Jones
Chief Design and R&D Officer, Newell Rubbermaid

Practitioner voice: his company opened a design center staffed with physiologists and perception experts. Studies unconscious behaviours and human cognitive processing. Emphasises packaging as a "ritual quality" — resistance, sound, tactile feel, the way it opens as "a series of disclosures."

Krishna, Lwin & Morrin
Nanyang Technological University / Rutgers University

Scented pencil study: pencils imbued with tea tree oil scent reduced recall decline over two weeks from 73% (unscented) to just 8%. Demonstrated that smell dramatically enhances memory encoding.

Key Moments & Consumer Behavior Concepts Illustrated ▾
CB Concept: Embodied Cognition / Priming

The Warm Beverage Study: Williams & Bargh had subjects briefly hold warm or cold drinks before evaluating a stranger. Warm-cup holders judged the stranger as friendlier. This illustrates how non-conscious sensory inputs (body warmth) prime social evaluations — directly relevant to in-store atmospherics, product packaging temperature, and even the feel of a handheld product.

CB Concept: Sensory Congruence & Cross-Modal Enhancement

The Cinnamon + Heating Pad Finding: Because cinnamon suggests warmth, adding a cinnamon scent enhanced a heating pad's perceived effectiveness. When two senses deliver congruent signals, each amplifies the other. For Oreo, the visual of dark chocolate and the actual bitter-sweet taste combination creates cross-modal reinforcement.

CB Concept: Olfactory Marketing / Classical Conditioning

Dunkin' Donuts South Korea: An atomiser released a coffee aroma whenever the brand's jingle played on municipal buses. Sales at outlets near bus stops increased 29%. This is classical conditioning (Pavlov) applied at scale — the jingle becomes a conditioned stimulus that triggers the olfactory memory and hunger cue.

CB Concept: Sensory Memory & Brand Recall

The Scented Pencil Study: Tea-tree-oil scented pencils produced only an 8% memory decline after two weeks versus a 73% decline for unscented pencils. This demonstrates the primacy of olfactory memory in long-term brand recall — with direct implications for flavoured and scented FMCG products like biscuits.

CB Concept: Tactile Ritual & Hedonic Value

Hershey's Kiss Foil Unwrapping: The tactile pleasure of unwrapping the foil transforms an ordinary piece of chocolate into a special experience. This is analogous to Oreo's "Twist, Lick, and Dunk" ritual — a designed tactile-gustatory sequence that creates hedonic and experiential value beyond the product itself.

CB Concept: Multisensory Product Design

BMW M5 Engine Sound: BMW mikes and amplifies engine sounds through speakers even when the audio system is turned off, enhancing the car's sporty feel. This is pure sensory engineering of a manufactured product attribute — sound has been decoupled from mechanical function to serve a pure hedonic/identity purpose. Automakers also optimise the feel of knobs, the solid noise of a door shutting, and the new-car smell.

Key Concepts & Frameworks ▾
Embodied Cognition

Without conscious awareness, bodily sensations help determine decisions. The body is not just a vehicle for the mind — it actively shapes perception and choice.

Sensory Congruence

When senses deliver signals that "match" in an expected way, they amplify one another. Incongruent or unexpected sensory combinations can either disrupt or pleasantly surprise.

Non-Conscious Processing

Sensory marketing works partly because consumers don't perceive it as marketing — and therefore don't activate cognitive defences against persuasion.

Olfactory Memory

Smell is processed by the limbic system and is directly linked to memory and emotion — making it the most powerful sensory trigger for brand recall.

Packaging as Ritual

The way a package opens, the sound it makes, the feel of its material — these create an experiential "unveiling" that shapes product perception before first use.

Holistic Brand Design

Every consumer company should design holistically across all five senses to create and intensify brand personalities that consumers will cherish and remember.

Managerial Implications ▾
  • Banking: Branch offices should exude reassuring aromas of wood and leather — sensory cues for wealth and trustworthiness.
  • Industrial products: Motors should produce solid, low-pitched hums, not tinny whines — sound communicates quality.
  • E-commerce / Luxury: Shipping in bubble wrap vs. high-quality crinkly paper sends very different brand messages.
  • FMCG / Biscuits: The crunch of the cookie, the aroma of chocolate, the visual of cream filling — each sense is a channel for brand communication, not just a functional property.

When Sensory Marketing Works and When it Backfires

Aparna Sundar & Theodore J. Noseworthy  |  University of Oregon & York University
▾
Brand PersonalitySincere vs Exciting BrandsSensory Expectations Packaging DesignSensory Tactics vs ManufacturingConsumer Preference
Overview & Central Argument ▾

Sensory marketing does not work uniformly for all brands. The effectiveness of a sensory tactic is highly dependent on a brand's perceived personality. Consumers intuitively link a brand's packaging and marketing tactics to their broader preconceptions of the brand's character — and they reward or punish brands whose sensory signals match or violate those expectations.

"For your packaging to evoke the right narrative, it must be designed intentionally. Consider how innovation in packaging would communicate to your consumer in the larger scheme of your brand's perceived personality. Above all, remember to design accordingly."
The Sincere vs. Exciting Framework ▾

Sincere Brands

Brand personality built on consistency, dependability, and trustworthiness. Consumers reward sensory tactics that deliver exactly what is expected. Unexpected sensory surprises are punished — even if objectively superior. Examples: Hallmark, Ford, Coca-Cola, Nokia, L.L. Bean

Exciting Brands

Brand personality built on innovation, energy, and surprise. Consumers reward sensory tactics that violate expectations and surprise them. Predictable sensory experiences feel flat. Examples: Mountain Dew, BMW, Pepsi, Apple, Nike

Key Finding: Punishment & Reward Effects

Consumers actually punished sincere brands by expressing less desire to purchase, even when a sensory tactic revealed superior quality (packaging looked plastic but was actually metal). Consumers rewarded exciting brands by expressing greater purchase desire, even when the sensory tactic revealed inferior quality (packaging looked metal but was actually plastic). The brand expectation, not the objective quality, was what mattered.

The Apple vs. Nokia Smartphone Study ▾

The authors built a new smartphone combining elements of the Apple iPhone 6 and Nokia Lumia 925. They marketed it alternately as an Apple or Nokia product, using each brand's logo. The phone looked plastic but could be made to feel like metal, cardboard fibre, or (as expected) plastic.

  • Apple (exciting brand): Consumers wanted the phone more when its promotional bumper case violated their sensory expectations — when assumed plastic but was metal or even cardboard (despite cardboard being inferior).
  • Nokia (sincere brand): Consumers wanted the phone more when its promotional tactic delivered exactly what was expected — a plastic bumper case — despite metal being objectively superior.
Critical Distinction: Tactic vs. Manufacturing

This effect only applied when sensory surprise was perceived as a brand tactic (packaging, promotional accessories). When the phone itself was made of different materials, consumers focused on objective quality regardless of brand. The inference that "the brand is choosing to surprise me" was what activated brand personality expectations.

Consumer Behavior Concepts Illustrated ▾
CB Concept: Brand Schema / Consumer Expectations

Consumers hold mental schemas about brand personalities. Any sensory input is evaluated against this schema. For sincere brands, schema-consistent cues produce positive affect; schema-violating cues produce negative affect even when the signal is objectively good. This is schema congruity theory applied to sensory marketing.

CB Concept: Shared Knowledge & Social Signalling

Consumer perceptions of brand personality often become shared knowledge — "everyone knows Luna bars are for women." This shared knowledge acts as a social norm that frames whether a sensory tactic feels appropriate or transgressive.

CB Concept: Heuristic Processing vs. Systematic Processing

When evaluating sensory marketing tactics (not manufacturing), consumers switch to heuristic processing — using brand personality as a mental shortcut to evaluate whether a sensory signal is "right for this brand." This explains why inferior material (cardboard) was accepted for Apple: the excitement heuristic overrode objective evaluation.

Oreo Application: Where Does Oreo Sit?

This article directly illuminates the Oreo India dilemma. Oreo's "Twist, Lick, and Dunk" ritual positions it as a sincere, family-oriented brand (consistent experience, predictable ritual, warmth). The case study confirms that consumers associated Oreo with sharing and collective consumption — sincere values. Sunfeast Dark Fantasy, by contrast, positioned itself as exciting and indulgent. The implication: sensory innovations (new formats, flavours, packaging surprises) work better for Dark Fantasy; consistent sensory ritual execution works better for Oreo. Any sensory tactic Oreo considers must reinforce its family-sharing identity, not violate it.

Key Concepts ▾
Brand Personality

The set of human characteristics associated with a brand. Aaker (1997) identified five dimensions: Sincerity, Excitement, Competence, Sophistication, Ruggedness.

Sensory Expectation Violation

When a sensory experience doesn't match what the consumer anticipated. Works positively for exciting brands; damages sincere brands.

Schema Congruity

The degree to which new information matches a consumer's existing mental model of a brand. High congruity = comfort; low congruity = either delight (exciting brands) or discomfort (sincere brands).

Tactic Attribution

The consumer inference that a sensory experience was deliberately chosen by the brand — as opposed to a neutral manufacturing decision. Only when a tactic is attributed to the brand does personality alignment become important.

Tactile Marketing

The deliberate use of touch (texture, weight, resistance, temperature) as a brand communication channel — from Coca-Cola's contoured bottle to Marfa's burlap packaging.

Multi-Sensory Brand Identity

The deliberate combination of sight, touch, sound, smell, and taste to create and maintain brand image or personality in a consumer's mind.

Sensory Branding: Oreo in the Indian Context

S Ramesh Kumar, Nalin Goel & Gireesh Gera  |  Indian Institute of Management Bangalore
▾
Sensory BrandingBrand RepositioningFunctional / Emotional / Symbolic Consumption Indian FMCGPremium BiscuitsConsumer Perception Twist Lick DunkBrand Personality
Overview & Central Problem ▾

This IIMB case study examines one of the most compelling real-world applications of sensory branding and consumer behavior theory in the Indian FMCG market. It is set in March 2015 — four years after Oreo's India launch — when the brand had achieved the remarkable position of India being its 3rd largest global market, yet Oreo's market share in the cream biscuit segment had stagnated at a constant 6% for two years.

The case centres on a single, high-stakes decision: should Chella Pandyan reposition Oreo? And if so, along which dimensions — functional, emotional, or symbolic — given the formidable competition from Sunfeast Dark Fantasy and Britannia Pure Magic?

The core research questions: How should Oreo differentiate itself from competitors? Should the brand reposition itself after it had created awareness in India? How should consumer perception be taken into consideration? — Chella Pandyan, Country Head (Biscuits), Mondelez India
Key Characters & Stakeholders ▾
Chella Pandyan
Country Head (Biscuits), Mondelez India Foods Private Limited

The central decision-maker of the case. He is the strategic protagonist who must navigate Oreo's stagnant 6% market share against deeply entrenched Indian competitors. He commissions both the 62-respondent survey and the six-person focus group, and must interpret their results to formulate a repositioning strategy. His dilemma is quintessentially a consumer behavior problem: how to shift perception without alienating the brand's established identity.

S Ramesh Kumar
Professor of Marketing, IIMB — Case Author

Brings the conceptual scaffolding of sensory branding, functional/emotional/symbolic consumption dimensions, and brand perception theory to the analysis of Oreo's situation in India.

The 62 Survey Respondents
Young adults (18–26), well-educated, post-graduate

The voice of the target consumer. They had seen and/or consumed all three brands. They scored each brand on 20 statements across functional, emotional, and symbolic dimensions on a 5-point Likert scale — producing the quantitative evidence Chella Pandyan needed.

The 6 Focus Group Participants
Subset of the 62 — qualitative insights

Their discussion revealed rich qualitative findings: the guilt of consuming calorie-dense biscuits, the inability to stop mid-pack, the pride of serving premium cream biscuits to guests, and critically — the perception that Oreo = sharing/collective consumption, while Dark Fantasy and Pure Magic = solitary self-indulgence.

Ranbir Kapoor
Bollywood Celebrity — Oreo Brand Ambassador (India)

Cast as a fun, youthful, aspirational figure to tap the young-adult market. His Air Force pilot advertisement (the "Ranbir and his sister" storyboard) embeds the Oreo brand in middle-class family playfulness and emotional bonding — reinforcing the sincere, relationship-oriented brand personality that differentiates Oreo from the more sensual/solitary positioning of its competitors.

The Beautiful Lady in the Castle
Dark Fantasy Advertisement Character

Not a real person, but the central character in the Sunfeast Dark Fantasy ad. She represents luxury, exclusivity, materialism, and sensual indulgence — seen in a satin dressing gown, surrounded by diamonds and exotic pets, eating the biscuit in a sensual manner. She embodies the "escape into one" fantasy that positions Dark Fantasy as solitary, aspirational, and hedonically exciting.

From Sensation to Perception: The Information-Processing Model ▾

Building on the classic Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968) information-processing model, consumer information processing unfolds in three connected stages: sensation (raw detection of a stimulus by the sensory receptors), attention (allocating limited mental resources to a subset of the stimuli sensed), and perception (organising and interpreting the attended stimuli into meaning). Each stage filters what survives to the next — most of what we sense, we never attend to; most of what we attend to, we interpret through the lens of prior knowledge rather than "as is."

Sensation

The immediate and direct response of the sensory organs (sensory receptors) to stimuli — the detection and encoding of a stimulus, before any meaning is attached to it.

Weber's Law & the Just Noticeable Difference ▾

The just noticeable difference (JND) is the minimal difference that can be detected between two similar stimuli — for example, the smallest step up from an intensity of 215 that a consumer will actually notice (in the classroom example, a JND of 35, i.e. up to 250).

Weber's Law

The ratio of the JND to the background (initial) intensity is a constant — the Weber fraction: JND / I = K, so JND ∝ I. The stronger the initial stimulus, the greater the additional intensity needed for a second stimulus to be perceived as different.

Weber's Law in Marketing

  • Negative changes (e.g. a price increase) should be kept below the JND so they go unnoticed.
  • Positive changes (e.g. a discount) should be kept above the JND so they are clearly noticed.
Classroom Example: Maggi's Price-Stealth vs. Kurkure's "10 Forever"

Maggi quietly raised its price in small steps — ₹10 (Jul 2021) → ₹12 (Aug 2021) → ₹14 (Jan 2023) — each increase small enough to stay below the JND. Kurkure, by contrast, made "10 Forever" a headline promise, deliberately keeping its price change imperceptible to protect the value perception. The same principle explains why logo redesigns are usually kept below the JND (a subtle refresh) rather than above it (a jarring change) — to protect consumers' sentimental attachment to the old mark.

Attention: What Consumers Notice — and Miss ▾

The McGurk effect (which organ is "really" responsible for hearing a sound, when what we see changes what we hear?) demonstrates that perception is multisensory and can be manipulated below conscious awareness — attention integrates cues across senses, not just within one.

Change Blindness

Occurs when an individual fails to notice an alteration in a scene, because of selective attention — famously demonstrated in the "door study," where a person changes entirely mid-conversation and most participants never notice.

Banner Blindness

A specific case of selective attention: the tendency to ignore page elements that a viewer perceives (correctly or incorrectly) to be advertisements. Eye-tracking heatmaps show fixations clustering everywhere except banner zones, with fast saccades skipping straight over them.

How Can Marketers Grab Consumers' Attention?

Color

Bright, "eye-catchy" colors draw the eye before any message is even read.

Location

Eye-level = buy-level; walking billboards exploit unexpected placement.

Novelty & Anomalies

Unusual, unexpected, or anomalous stimuli break through habituated scanning patterns.

Relevance

Something personally relevant to the viewer's goals or identity earns attention.

Curiosity

Teaser advertising withholds information deliberately to provoke continued attention.

Familiarity

Personalised messages ("something familiar") cut through clutter by referencing the self.

Perception: Giving Meaning to Sensation ▾

Perception is the process by which individuals select, organise, and interpret stimuli — giving meaning to sensation. Perceptions are subjective understandings, not objective realities: Perception = f(stimulus, situation/context, person/prior knowledge). The same saree can be judged as costing anywhere from ₹4,560 to ₹14,648 depending purely on contextual cues, with no change to the product itself.

Schema Theory

A schema is a mental structure that helps us organise and interpret information. New information can be processed in three ways:

  • Assimilation — fitting new information into an existing schema (e.g. Cherry Coke slots neatly into the existing "Coke" schema).
  • Accommodation — modifying an existing schema to fit new information (e.g. "District by Zomato" stretches the food-delivery schema to include dine-out experiences).
  • Creation of a New Schema — when information doesn't fit any existing category at all (e.g. Comet, a genuinely new product form).
How Marketing Cues Silently Shape Perception ▾

Visual Placement

Product images placed on the bottom and right side of a package make the product appear visually heavier; images on the top and left create a perception of being visually lighter (Deng & Kahn, 2009). Consumers prefer powerful brands more when the logo sits high on the packaging, and prefer less powerful brands more when the logo sits low.

Sound Symbolism

The sound of a word or brand name can convey meaning beyond its literal definition. Brand names with front vowel sounds (e.g. Nellen, Frish) are perceived as smaller, lighter, milder, thinner, softer, faster, colder, friendlier, and prettier than names with back vowel sounds (e.g. Nullen, Frosh) (Klink, 2000) — which is why "Bugatti" sounds faster than "Rimac" even before either car is seen. Real brand names lean on this: KitKat (crunchiness), Swiggy (swiftness), Zoomcar (speed/motion).

Fonts Matter Too

Serif fonts read as stable, practical, mature, and formal; script fonts read as youthful, happy, creative, rebellious, and casual; sans-serif fonts read as neutral — neither high nor low on any personality trait.

Logo Shape & Complexity

A circular logo creates perceptions of being softer, more comfortable, warmer, and more sensitive to customer needs than an angular logo, which reads as harder, more durable (Jiang et al., 2016). A more complex logo makes a brand appear more luxurious than a simpler one (Tang et al., 2025). Firms with incomplete typeface logos are perceived as less trustworthy but more innovative (Hagtvedt, 2011).

Brand Extension & Perceived Congruity ▾

How congruent should a brand extension be with the parent brand? The classroom gallery of extensions ranges from triumph to disaster: Ferrari Luxury Clothing (a success), Harley-Davidson Perfume and its Cake Decorating Kit (bigger failures), the Cheetos-flavoured Lip Balm (a debacle), and the Burger King Cologne (a limited-edition novelty).

The Congruity Spectrum (Jhang et al., 2012)

High congruity — fails to evoke arousal, resulting in only mildly positive, familiarity-based evaluations. Moderate incongruity — evokes arousal, but the consumer can resolve it, producing the most favourable evaluations. Extreme incongruity — evokes arousal but is too difficult to resolve, leading to relatively negative evaluations.

Tools for Uncovering Consumer Perceptions ▾

Because perceptions are subjective and often held below conscious awareness, marketers rely on projective techniques to surface them indirectly:

Word Association

The first word that comes to mind when the respondent thinks of the brand.

Sentence Completion

e.g. "When I think of [brand], I …" — completions reveal latent associations.

Personification / Animism

If the brand came to life as a person or animal, how would you describe it? Used to surface brand personality.

Obituary Technique

Imagine the brand died — what would its obituary say? Or: if reborn, what would it look like? (useful for brand extension).

Symbolic Analysis

Analyse symbolic meaning by comparing a product to its opposite (e.g. "what would non-usage of beer feel like?") to elicit deep, less expressible meanings.

ZMET (Zaltman Metaphor Elicitation Technique)

Participants bring photos representing their thoughts and feelings about a brand (Zaltman & Coulter, 1995) — used to access non-verbal, metaphor-based brand associations.

Key Concepts & Frameworks ▾
Just Noticeable Difference (JND)

The minimal difference between two stimuli that is detectable — governed by Weber's Law (JND ∝ I).

Selective Attention

Consumers allocate limited attentional resources selectively, producing phenomena like change blindness and banner blindness.

Schema Theory

Mental structures (schemas) that organise information via assimilation, accommodation, or the creation of an entirely new schema.

Sound Symbolism

Phonetic properties of brand names (e.g. vowel sound) non-consciously convey size, speed, warmth, and other traits.

Congruity Spectrum

Moderate incongruity in brand extensions outperforms both high congruity and extreme incongruity.

Projective Techniques

Indirect research methods (word association, ZMET, obituary, etc.) that surface perceptions consumers cannot or will not state directly.

Ads Implant False Memories

Jonah Lehrer  |  Science, WIRED
▾
False Experience EffectMemory ReconsolidationHigh-Imagery Advertising Episodic MemoryProduct Placement in the Brain
Overview & Central Argument ▾

Lehrer opens with his own vivid, "cinematic" high-school memory of drinking Coca-Cola from vintage glass bottles at a Friday-night football game — a memory he is certain is false, since his school had banned glass containers. He traces the scene to a Coca-Cola commercial he'd likely watched, one of the ads in which "the marketing message is less about the virtues of the product... and more about associating the drink with a set of intensely pleasurable memories." A new study in the Journal of Consumer Research explains exactly how vivid advertising can trick the brain into believing a fictional scene actually happened to us.

The Study: A Popcorn That Never Existed ▾

Researchers Priyali Rajagopal and Nicole Montgomery introduced 100 undergraduates to a fictional product: "Orville Redenbacher's Gourmet Fresh Microwave Popcorn" (no such product exists). Subjects were randomly assigned to a low-imagery text ad describing the taste, or a high-imagery commercial showing happy people enjoying the popcorn. Afterward, some subjects were given an unrelated survey; others were given an actual (different) popcorn sample to taste.

One Week Later

Subjects who saw the low-imagery ad rarely reported having tried the popcorn. But subjects who watched the vivid, high-imagery commercial were just as likely to report having tried the (nonexistent) popcorn as those who actually had — and rated it just as favourably. They were extremely confident in these entirely fabricated memories.

The False Experience Effect ▾

The researchers call this the false experience effect — vivid ads slyly weave fictional experiences into consumers' real autobiographical lives.

"Viewing the vivid advertisement created a false memory of eating the popcorn, despite the fact that eating the non-existent product would have been impossible. As a result, consumers need to be vigilant while processing high-imagery advertisements." — Priyali Rajagopal & Nicole Montgomery
Why Does This Happen? Memory Reconsolidation ▾

The explanation lies in memory reconsolidation: every time we recall a memory, we also remake it, subtly rewriting its neuronal details. Memories are not immutable impressions sealed off from the act of remembering them — "a memory is only as real as the last time you remembered it." Because we constantly borrow memories from elsewhere, an ad watched on television can become absorbed into our own personal narrative.

"It's the difference between a 'Save' and the 'Save As' function. Our memories are a 'Save As': they are files that get rewritten every time we remember them, which is why the more we remember something, the less accurate the memory becomes."
Key Concepts & Marketing Implications ▾
False Experience Effect

Vivid, high-imagery advertising can create a false memory of having experienced (tried, tasted, used) a product, even when that experience never happened.

Memory Reconsolidation

Every act of recall rewrites the memory, allowing ad-derived scenes to be absorbed into a consumer's own autobiographical narrative.

High- vs. Low-Imagery Advertising

Only high-imagery (vivid, scene-based) ads produced the false experience effect — text-only, low-imagery ads did not.

Episodic Memory

Autobiographical memory of specific personal events — the type of memory ads can implant and distort.

For marketers, this is a striking implication of emotionally vivid, imagery-rich advertising: it can do more than build preference — it can (unintentionally or otherwise) build a false sense of first-hand product experience, which is one reason vivid storytelling ads can be so much more persuasive than fact-based ones.

We Can't Recall Logos We See Every Day

An Interview with Alan Castel (UCLA) by Alison Beard  |  Harvard Business Review
▾

The Research: Drawing the Apple Logo From Memory ▾

UCLA professor Alan Castel and colleagues asked more than 100 students to draw the Apple logo from memory. Most were Mac or iPhone users and were reasonably confident they could do it accurately — yet only one person drew it correctly. When asked instead to pick the real logo out of a lineup that included seven altered variations, fewer than half (47%) chose correctly.

The challenge: is our visual memory really that terrible? How could it be so hard to remember something so simple?
Why Visual Memory Fails Us for Familiar Objects ▾

Castel's response: we actually have excellent visual memory — good enough to recognise complex pictures, like other people's vacation photos, that we've only seen once. But we also face attentional saturation: it would be overwhelming and maladaptive to mentally record every detail of everything we see, so we subconsciously let most of it fall away, retaining only what's critical.

Subjects were confident about the Apple logo because of the availability heuristic: "I've seen this many times, so I should remember it" — a felt sense of familiarity mistaken for actual encoded detail. The same failure shows up for pennies (which way Lincoln faces), calculator keypads, computer keyboards, elevator buttons, and road signs — familiar, simple, ubiquitous objects whose details we never actually study.

The Fire Extinguisher Studies & Gist Memory ▾

In a follow-up study, only 13 of 54 people in one office building could correctly recall the location of the fire extinguisher nearest their desk — despite it being "big and red" and safety-critical. Confidence was low too — an example of good metacognition (knowing what you don't know). By contrast, 96% of people in the same building could locate the nearest drinking fountain, because they used it regularly.

Castel attributes some of the extinguisher failure to gist memory — believing you know roughly where something "should" be (e.g. near the elevator) rather than actually recalling where it is — and to inattentional amnesia, where people are too preoccupied with other tasks to notice something in plain sight (as in the famous "gorilla in the basketball game" study).

Failure Is a Good Teacher

Two months after the initial fire-extinguisher test — having failed once and looked it up — every single subject correctly remembered the location the second time. A single meaningful failure encoded the information far better than years of daily, inattentive exposure ever did.

Is Having a Bad Memory Actually a Good Thing? ▾

Forgetting low-value information (where you parked last week, a name you'll never need again) frees up mental capacity for higher-value information. People who are especially good at retaining everything can actually suffer more from interference — remembering a trivial conversation but not where they left their car keys. In a related study on drug side effects, subjects were reliably good at remembering the severe risks (e.g. stroke) but not minor ones (e.g. swelling feet) — even though minor symptoms can sometimes signal something serious, meaning we must be careful about how much we filter out.

Key Concepts & Business Implications ▾
Availability Heuristic

Mistaking frequent exposure for accurate memory — "I've seen it, so I must remember it."

Attentional Saturation

The brain avoids the maladaptive burden of encoding every detail of familiar, ubiquitous objects.

Gist Memory

Recalling the general schema of where/what something is, rather than its actual specific detail or location.

Inattentional Amnesia

Failing to notice or encode something because attention was consumed by another task.

Metacognition

Knowing what you don't know — low confidence in the fire-extinguisher study correctly signalled poor recall.

For marketers, familiarity is not the same as memorability: a logo or brand mark seen daily can still be poorly encoded in memory. In high-stakes settings (criminal, medical, business), relying on consumers' gist memory or inattentional recall can be risky — distinctiveness and encouraging active engagement (not just repeated exposure) build more accurate, durable memory.

Overview: Why Most New Products Fail ▾

Despite billions invested in innovation, 40% to 90% of new products fail — a rate unchanged in 25 years. Gourville cites Webvan ($1 billion lost before its 2001 bankruptcy), Segway (6,000 units sold vs. 50,000–100,000 projected), and TiVo ($600 million in operating losses by 2005 despite rave reviews) as high-profile examples. The usual explanation — "bad idea" — is too simple: many of these products were objectively superior to what they replaced.

Gourville's answer lies in behavioral psychology, not product economics: new products require behavior change, and consumers and companies are both systematically biased about the value of that change — in opposite directions.

Many innovations fail because consumers irrationally overvalue the old and companies irrationally overvalue the new.
The Psychology of Gains and Losses ▾

Drawing on Kahneman and Tversky's Nobel Prize–winning work, Gourville notes that people evaluate alternatives against a reference point — usually the product they already own — treating improvements as gains and shortcomings as losses. Crucially, losses loom larger than equivalent gains ("loss aversion"): most people won't accept a 50/50 bet to win or lose $100, and Pacific Gas & Electric customers demanded 3–4x more compensation to endure a power outage than they'd pay to avoid one.

Endowment Effect

Richard Thaler's term for overvaluing what you already own. In his coffee-mug experiment, "Sellers" priced mugs at ~$7, while "Choosers" (offered the identical choice) valued the same mug at only ~$3.50 — Sellers demanded roughly twice as much to give up what they had.

Status Quo Bias

Jack Knetsch gave students a coffee mug or a chocolate bar, then let them trade. Only ~10–11% swapped — even though, given a free choice upfront, the split was nearly 50/50. Giving up what you have feels like a painful loss.

People are largely unaware of these biases in themselves — when shown evidence of their own status-quo overvaluation, they tend to be shocked, skeptical, and defensive.

The 9x Effect ▾

Consumers overvalue an entrenched product's benefits by a factor of roughly three (loss aversion / endowment effect). Companies, meanwhile, are just as biased in the opposite direction: having lived with their innovation for months or years, developers treat it as the reference point and overvalue its new benefits by a factor of roughly three as well — a bias Gourville attributes partly to the "curse of knowledge" (once you know something, you can't easily imagine not knowing it, so you assume others will value what you value).

The 9x Mismatch

3× (consumers overweighting the incumbent) × 3× (companies overweighting the innovation) = a 9-to-1 gap between what innovators believe consumers want and what consumers actually want. Left unmanaged, this mismatch is a recipe for the kind of disappointment Webvan, Segway, and TiVo experienced.

The Behavior-Change Matrix ▾

Companies create value through product change but capture it most easily by minimizing behavior change required of the consumer — a tension Gourville maps onto a 2×2 matrix.

QuadrantDescriptionExample
Easy SellsLimited product change, limited behavior changeAngled-head toothbrushes — high adoption, limited upside

Managing Resistance: Accept, or Minimize ▾

For unavoidable "Long Haul" innovations, Gourville recommends accepting resistance: be patient and don't burn resources expecting fast adoption (TiVo's fate vs. the DVD player, which fit seamlessly into VCR-era habits); strive for a 10x improvement so gains swamp perceived losses (MRIs over X-rays, angioplasty over bypass surgery); or, where possible, eliminate the old alternative entirely (Canada's loonie succeeded because bills were withdrawn from circulation — the US dollar coin repeatedly failed because the dollar bill wasn't).

Where the long haul is unattractive, companies should instead work to minimize resistance:

Behaviorally Compatible Products

Toyota's Prius pairs a self-charging electric engine with a conventional one, so driving feels identical to a gas-only car while mileage improves — the first alt-fuel vehicle to win mass US acceptance (100,000+ sold in 2005).

Seek Out the Unendowed

Burton Snowboards targeted young winter-sports enthusiasts who hadn't yet become skiers, rather than trying to convert entrenched skiers — growing snowboarding from nothing to outnumbering skiing in the US.

Find Believers

Hydrogen fuel-cell makers can target consumers who prize the new benefit or barely miss what they'd give up — e.g. islanders in Iceland or Bermuda, for whom a sparse hydrogen-station network matters less than it would to mainlanders.

Regulators can also help eliminate the old: emissions standards or reimbursement rules can effectively phase out incumbent products even when a company can't do so directly.

Key Takeaway ▾

It's not enough for a new product to be objectively better — unless the gains far outweigh the losses, as perceived through the consumer's biased lens, adoption will stall. Companies must diagnose how much behavior change their innovation demands, then either accept and manage the resulting resistance or actively engineer it away.

Implicit Predictors of Consumer Behavior

Nancy M. Puccinelli, Kathryn Braun & Fred Mast, under Prof. Gerald Zaltman  |  Harvard Business School
▾

Implicit vs. Explicit Knowledge ▾

Asked to describe a familiar brand like Coca-Cola, most people can generate a handful of adjectives — but this list captures only a fraction of their actual associations with the brand, many formed by childhood ads, game-day exposure, or a can drunk earlier that day, and sitting outside conscious awareness. Psychology distinguishes explicit knowledge (consciously held beliefs, drawing on remembered experience) from implicit knowledge (cognitive associations that exist outside conscious awareness).

The clearest evidence of the split comes from amnesiac patients: a doctor who shakes a patient's hand with a hidden pin in her palm finds the patient later refuses to shake her hand again — despite having no explicit memory of being pricked. An unconscious, implicit attitude persisted where explicit memory could not.

Measuring the Unconscious: Priming and the IAT ▾

Explicit measures are the marketer's familiar tool: paper-and-pencil surveys asking how much someone likes a brand, or whether they recall a specific ad. Implicit measures rely on observable behavior instead — chiefly the priming paradigm, where a stimulus (e.g. a Coca-Cola bottle) immediately precedes a target word (e.g. "refreshing"), and faster reaction times reveal a stronger underlying association.

Implicit Association Test (IAT)

Measures the relative strength of association between two concept pairs (e.g. flowers/insects and pleasant/unpleasant) via reaction-time differences — later adapted to test how positively or negatively consumers associate specific brands.

Neural Basis

Repeated exposure produces measurably decreased brain activation in visual and frontal-lobe regions tied to perceptual and semantic processing — regions typically undamaged in amnesiacs, explaining why priming effects survive even without explicit memory.

Can Things We're Unaware Of Influence Behavior? ▾

A long line of social-psychology research says yes. Essays were rated lower when a female name appeared in the byline, despite no evaluator consciously endorsing that bias (Goldberg, 1968). In "lost letter" studies, passersby were more likely to mail a stranger's dropped letter if they held favorable implicit attitudes toward the addressed organization. White interviewers unconsciously kept greater physical distance and made less eye contact with Black candidates than White ones.

Mere exposure experiments found people prefer polygons they'd been shown too briefly to consciously perceive; fictitious brand names became "famous overnight" purely through unremembered exposure. In one advertising study, ads flanking a magazine article had no effect on an explicit recognition test, yet significantly increased whether the advertised product later showed up in participants' purchase-consideration sets — the implicit test caught what the explicit test missed entirely.

When Implicit Tests Are Most Revealing ▾

The gap between implicit and explicit measures is widest when the explicit answer feels socially unacceptable to admit. Explicit self-reports of racial prejudice showed a near-zero correlation with IAT-measured implicit attitudes (Greenwald, McGhee & Schwartz, 1998) — people either couldn't or wouldn't report the bias. By contrast, attitudes toward something low-stakes like computer brands showed high correlation between implicit and explicit measures, since there's little social cost to admitting you're a Mac person.

Implicit attitudes, judged from behavior outside a person's conscious control, are less susceptible to demand characteristics and the motivation to make a good impression — under some circumstances they better reflect a person's honest belief.
Implicit Associations with Brands ▾

Consumers hold real stereotypes about brand users — Nescafe (instant) drinkers were rated "lazy" and "poor planners" relative to Maxwell House (drip) drinkers (Haire, 1950), despite no consumer consciously endorsing that judgment. Consumer ethnocentrism offers a sharp case study: US and foreign MBA students both reported very low explicit ethnocentrism on the CETSCALE survey, yet the IAT told a different story — US students were faster pairing US brands with positive traits than foreign brands, while the correlation between the explicit CETSCALE and the implicit IAT was r = -.18, meaning the survey didn't just miss the bias, it would have actively misled marketers about it. A parallel study of Milk Bar (local) vs. McDonald's (global) in Poland found consumers explicitly preferred whichever brand they actually used, but showed an implicit preference for Milk Bar regardless.

Comparing Coke and Pepsi drinkers, both groups explicitly preferred their own brand — but only Coke drinkers showed strong positive implicit associations, suggesting Coke's brand relationship is more deeply and durably encoded, likely through decades of advertising and lived experience. A separate study found consumer mood moderates implicit brand associations: positive associations with Ben & Jerry's and negative associations with Marlboro were both stronger among sad consumers, and while implicit measures predicted explicit attitudes well for happy consumers (r = .66), they predicted the opposite for sad consumers (r = -.66).

Implicit Measures in Sales-Force Management ▾

Implicit self-esteem (measured indirectly) predicted interview anxiety independent of explicit self-esteem scores on a standard questionnaire — suggesting implicit batteries could help identify candidates suited to high-pressure negotiations. Separately, research on "multiple identities" found that subtle situational cues can prime different aspects of a person's identity: an Asian woman asked about her language background (priming her Asian identity) performed better on a quantitative test than when asked about coed dorms (priming her female identity) — implying that even the framing of a sales conversation can prime a customer identity that shapes their subsequent choices.

Implicit Knowledge

Cognitive associations that exist and influence behavior outside conscious awareness.

Priming

Exposure to a stimulus that speeds up (or biases) response to a related target stimulus.

Mere Exposure Effect

People prefer things merely because they've encountered them before, even without conscious recognition of the prior exposure.

Consumer Ethnocentrism

An implicit preference for home-country brands over foreign ones — often denied or unrecognized on explicit surveys.

For marketers, the takeaway is methodological: explicit surveys alone risk missing — or actively misrepresenting — the associations that actually drive purchase behavior, particularly wherever social desirability is in play.

The Uses (and Abuses) of Influence

An Interview with Robert Cialdini by Sarah Cliffe  |  Harvard Business Review
▾
Six Principles of PersuasionReciprocitySocial Proof AuthorityThe Power of "We"Ethics of Influence
Overview ▾

Robert Cialdini — Regents' Professor Emeritus of Psychology and Marketing at Arizona State University — is the researcher behind six well-documented principles of persuasion, first laid out in his 2001 book Influence. In this HBR interview, editor Sarah Cliffe runs a series of everyday workplace scenarios by him to draw out how the principles apply in practice — and probes newer research on the ethics of influence.

Getting Resources: Reciprocity Requires Prework ▾

For an employee trying to secure resources for a new idea, Cialdini's advice starts before the ask: help people first, and — critically — never wave off their thanks with "no big deal." Instead, label the exchange as partnership ("it's what partners do for each other"), because we hold significant persuasive power immediately after being thanked.

Frank Flynn's research at a large telecom found that employees who helped colleagues were seen as highly valuable but had lower personal productivity from time diverted to others' problems. The variable that improved both social value and productivity wasn't the number of favors given — it was the number of favors exchanged: framing help as a two-way partnership, not one-way generosity.

Leading Change Under Uncertainty ▾

People facing uncertainty freeze, fearing loss. Per Kahneman, notions of loss are psychologically more powerful than notions of gain — so leaders should frame change not only in terms of what will be gained, but what will be lost by staying put. Uncertain people also stop looking inward and start looking to two external sources: authority (not necessarily the boss, but whoever knows the subject best — "being an authority" vs. "being in authority") and peers — a manager should get a respected, agreeing team member to speak up rather than pressuring holdouts directly, since peers are often more convincing than executives.

Locking In Commitment: The Power of "We" ▾

When people see themselves as part of a group with shared identity, they'll take steps they wouldn't take for individual interest alone — but that sense of shared purpose fades once people disperse back to their everyday lives. Cialdini's fix: get a public, and ideally written, commitment while the group is still together. "People live up to what they write down" — it seems to make the choice more conscious — and asking for a concrete next step and a follow-up date makes the commitment progressively more concrete.

Networks, Advocates, and the Liking Principle ▾

Building an informal network starts with finding genuine commonalities (shared hobbies, schools) — we like people who are like us, and honest similarity-seeking builds mutual liking. For negotiating on one's own behalf, Cialdini and Jeffrey Pfeffer found real value in using an advocate: it signals prestige (the authority principle) and avoids the "braggart" penalty that self-promotion incurs (the liking principle). That penalty falls disproportionately on women — self-advocacy that damages a woman's likability barely dents a man's — making organizations where managers are expected to advocate for their people especially valuable for closing this gap. For minority or "other" employees, surface similarity (race, ethnicity, origin) may be absent, but shared values can substitute — and are best surfaced by talking about priorities and values more spontaneously.

Cross-Cultural and Digital Shifts in Persuasion ▾

All six principles appear to exist across cultures, but their relative weight shifts. In more collectivist, communal cultures, social proof carries more force: a US/Poland study of survey participation found US willingness best predicted by one's own past behavior (consistency), while Polish willingness was best predicted by friends' past behavior (social proof). A Citibank study of manager helping-behavior found Hong Kong and China managers asking "is the requester connected to someone senior?" (fealty/authority), while Spanish managers asked "is the requester connected to a friend of mine?" (liking/loyalty).

Online, social media hasn't changed how people respond to persuasion appeals, but it has shifted whose appeals count — platforms like TripAdvisor and Yelp show peers, not experts, now driving choices. In Cialdini's own hotel-towel-reuse research, the most effective message wasn't "most guests at this hotel reuse towels" but the more localized "most guests in this room reuse towels" — the more personalized the social proof, the more powerful it is.

Durability and the Ethics of Influence ▾

A multi-year study with the energy company Opower found that giving households ongoing information about neighbors' energy usage produced usage changes that persisted over years — durability requires giving people a standing reason to keep paying attention, not a one-time nudge.

On ethics, Cialdini notes people rarely self-restrain because they don't expect to get caught — "especially at the highest levels of power, people feel bulletproof." His newer research instead makes a bottom-line case for honesty: employees uncomfortable with a dishonest culture tend to leave, while those comfortable with it stay, so a dishonest organization gradually fills with people who are comfortable cheating — including cheating the organization itself. In experiments with Adriana Samper and Jessica Li, team members who merely believed their teammates had conspired to cheat performed significantly worse on a difficult problem (stress/preoccupation), and people who chose to work with a dishonest team went on to cheat 50% more often themselves.

"It's just clear that the more localized and personalized we can make a source of information, the more likely it is to move people in our direction." — Robert Cialdini, on why the "most primitive" techniques of influence remain the most powerful.
The Six Principles of Persuasion ▾
Liking

People say yes more readily to people they like — because those people like them, or because of things they have in common.

Reciprocity

People return favors. Help others, and behave the way you'd like them to behave, and they tend to respond in kind.

Social Proof

People do what they see similar others doing — especially powerful in collectivist cultures and in localized, personalized framing.

Commitment & Consistency

People want to be — or at least appear — consistent. A public, voluntary commitment (especially written) is one they'll work to fulfil.

Authority

People defer to experts and to those in positions of authority, typically underestimating how much they do so.

Scarcity

People value things more when they perceive them to be scarce or limited.

Consumer Decision Making: Heuristics, Biases & Choice Architecture

Overview: The Consumer Decision-Making Process ▾

When a decision is deliberate and effortful — planning a 30th-birthday party, say — consumers move through a five-stage System 2 process: problem recognition → information search → evaluation of alternatives → purchase decision → post-purchase evaluation. But most everyday decisions (where to grab lunch with friends) never engage this full sequence at all — they're settled almost instantly by intuition. Which route a decision takes depends on a distinction, coined by psychologists Keith Stanovich and Richard West and popularised by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, between two systems of thinking.

The Five-Stage System 2 Process

1. Problem Recognition — 2. Information Search — 3. Evaluation of Alternatives — 4. Purchase Decision — 5. Post-Purchase Evaluation. Reserved for decisions that are effortful, high-involvement, or unfamiliar.

System 1 vs. System 2 ▾
System 1

Automatic, intuitive, and quick, with little or no effort; driven by instinct; no sense of voluntary control.

System 2

Conscious and logical; involves attention and effortful mental computation; associated with agency, choice, and concentration; overcomes System 1's impulses through self-control.

What determines which system gets used? Familiarity and expertise, perceived risk and stakes, time pressure, and personal involvement all push a decision toward one system or the other. Buying a laptop during a planned sale (System 2 — extensive search) looks completely different from replacing a laptop that died two days before a deadline (System 1 — fast, constrained choice), even though the product category is identical. High-stakes, unfamiliar decisions (choosing a life-insurance policy, a legal attorney) pull toward System 2 — though even here, thin-slicing judgements (snap impressions formed from very limited information, often accurate but bias-prone — Ambady & Rosenthal, 1992) can quietly substitute for real deliberation, as when a voter picks a Mess Committee president on a few seconds' impression.

Information Search: Novices, the Moderately Knowledgeable & Experts ▾

How much a consumer searches before buying depends on their existing expertise (Solomon & Panda, 2020):

Novice

Limited expertise; feels less competent to search; visits one store, sticks to familiar brands, focuses on a few features, and leans on others' opinions and "nonfunctional" cues like brand name and price.

Moderately Knowledgeable

Has some expertise, feels competent to search, and — counterintuitively — searches the most of the three groups.

Expert

Good product knowledge, a sharp sense of what information is actually relevant, so search is selective rather than exhaustive — leading to a well-informed choice with less total effort.

Heuristics & Cognitive Biases: Why We Take Shortcuts ▾

Heuristics are mental shortcuts — rules of thumb for snap judgements — that exist because consumers are cognitive misers: lazy, inattentive minds that default to natural, non-deliberative assessment rather than full evaluation. Everyday marketplace heuristics include: a foreign-sounding brand name signals luxury/premiumness; an international brand signals good quality; high demand signals a good product; a higher rating signals a better product; a higher price signals better quality; "healthy" signals less tasty and more expensive; and a steep discount signals a product nearing expiry or slow-moving.

None of these inferences are guaranteed to be true — they're shortcuts that are usually good enough, not always correct, which is exactly what makes them exploitable by both marketers and consumers' own snap judgements.
Anchoring & Adjustment ▾

Anchoring and adjustment is a heuristic that involves relying heavily on the first piece of information (the "anchor") encountered when making an estimate, then adjusting insufficiently away from it.

Classroom Demonstration

Two groups estimated a Bengaluru engineer's monthly salary. Asked "is it above or below ₹85,000?", the group's average estimate landed near ₹98,143. Asked "is it above or below ₹1,35,000?" instead — same engineer, same facts — the average estimate jumped to ₹1,63,421. The only thing that changed was the anchor.

The Decoy Effect & the Compromise Effect ▾

The decoy effect: preference for option X over Y can be increased by adding a third alternative that is clearly inferior to X but not clearly inferior to Y (Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982, on asymmetrically dominated alternatives).

Classroom Demonstration — The Salon Menu

Choosing between X: Haircut (₹400) and Y: Haircut + Wash + Styling (₹700), many pick X. Add a decoy — Z: Haircut + Wash (₹650), worse than Y on every dimension but only slightly cheaper — and Y suddenly looks like the clear win, pulling choice share toward it.

The related compromise effect (Simonson, 1989): consumers are more likely to choose the option that represents a middle ground between two more extreme alternatives — the "safe," moderate choice becomes easier to justify to oneself than either extreme.

The Availability Heuristic ▾

The availability heuristic: judging the frequency or probability of an event by the ease with which instances come to mind. Recent or vivid experiences distort probability judgment — asked whether more seven-letter English words end in "_ing" or have "n" as the sixth letter, most people say "_ing" is more common, even though every "_ing" word necessarily also has "n" in the sixth position, simply because "_ing" endings are easier to recall.

The same distortion shapes real judgements: which airline feels "safer," Air India or IndiGo, often tracks which one's incidents were more recently or vividly covered, not actual safety statistics. Framing a life-insurance pitch around a vivid, available risk (a stroke) versus a less imagined one (a road accident) changes perceived relevance regardless of actual base rates — and consumers likewise over- or under-estimate real causes of death in India based on which cause gets more vivid media coverage (Khatri et al., 2021). The same heuristic drives how shoppers read product reviews and social-media chatter as signals of a brand's real quality.

Choice Overload / Hyperchoice ▾

Choice overload (hyperchoice): the complexity of a decision problem exceeds an individual's cognitive resources to handle it — the basis of Barry Schwartz's paradox of choice, where consumers say they want more options even though more options make them measurably worse off (harder to decide, less satisfied with what they pick, e.g. "Mutual Funds Sahi Hai. Par, Bahut Hai!" — so many mutual fund options that "sahi" (correct) search becomes its own burden).

What Is the Optimum Number of Choices?

There's no universal magic number, but studies suggest 8–15 options is a common sweet spot — though the real optimum depends on the shopper's Ability, Motivation, and Opportunity (AMO) to process the choice set (Chernev et al., 2015, on the antecedents and consequences of choice overload).

The Role of Affect: Loss Aversion & the Framing Effect ▾

Loss aversion: the pain of losing is psychologically more powerful than the pleasure of an equivalent gain — we dislike losing more than we like winning, and take on more risk specifically to avoid a loss.

Classroom Demonstration — Gain vs. Loss Framing

Given ₹2,000 already in hand, most people choose the safe "100% chance to get ₹900" over the riskier "90% chance to get ₹1,000." But reframed as losses — "100% chance to lose ₹900" vs. "90% chance to lose ₹1,000" — most people flip to the riskier option, taking on more risk purely to avoid a certain loss, even though the underlying numbers are identical.

The framing effect: decisions are shaped by how information is presented, not just its substance — people systematically prefer positively framed options over negatively framed ones. A surgery framed as "50% of patients survive" reads far more favourably than the identical "50% of patients die," even though the odds are exactly the same.

Pricing Psychology: Pain of Paying, Zero Price & Mental Accounting ▾

The pain of paying — the psychological discomfort of parting with money — explains why consumers love discounts (a reduction in that pain) and overvalue freebies far beyond their objective worth. The zero price effect captures this: dropping a price to exactly ₹0 triggers a disproportionate jump in demand that a merely "very cheap" price never does, because free eliminates any sense of loss entirely.

Classroom Demonstration — Windfall vs. Salary

An unexpected ₹2,000 won in a mall lucky draw tends to get spent on something indulgent or fun. The same ₹2,000, credited as an unexpected salary correction from an employer, tends to get folded into planned, routine spending. Same amount, same "unexpectedness" — but a different mental accounting bucket, based purely on where the money seemed to come from.

Mental accounting: a cognitive bias where people divide money into separate imaginary buckets based on its source or intended use, treating a rupee from one bucket as less fungible than the theory of money would predict. Two related, ownership-driven biases round this out: the endowment effect (people value what they already own more highly than they would if they didn't own it — sellers routinely price an item above what it would fetch elsewhere), and the IKEA effect (a disproportionately high value placed on products the consumer helped create or assemble themselves).

The Affect Heuristic & Mood ▾

The affect heuristic: the emotions elicited by the options under consideration — how a choice simply feels — govern decision-making more than a cool cost-benefit calculation does. Positive emotion reads as high benefit/low risk; negative emotion reads as low benefit/high risk.

Mood also shapes the style of thinking, not just the valence of judgement. A positive mood tends to focus attention on the big picture and the long term (favouring, e.g., healthier consumption choices); a negative mood narrows focus toward immediate gains (favouring more indulgent, in-the-moment choices).

Different negative moods don't all behave the same way (George & Dane, 2016; Fessler, Pillsworth & Flamson, 2004):

EmotionBehavioral Output
Sadness (sense of loss)Risk-seeking; higher purchases ("retail therapy")
Anxiety / Fear (lack of control, uncertainty)Risk-aversion

Sadness in particular makes people pay more and consume more — the psychological basis of "retail therapy" as a real, documented spending pattern rather than just a figure of speech.
A Few Other Cognitive Biases ▾
Status Quo / Default Bias

A preference for things to stay the same — pre-set defaults are disproportionately "sticky" because changing them requires an active, effortful decision.

Present Bias / Hyperbolic Discounting

Rewards available right now are valued much more heavily than equivalent (or larger) rewards in the future.

Goal Gradient Effect

Effort and motivation intensify as a person perceives themselves getting closer to a goal — e.g. a loyalty-card punch-card that speeds up completion near the end.

Key Concepts & Choice Architecture Implications ▾
System 1 / System 2

Fast, automatic judgement vs. slow, effortful, deliberate reasoning — most marketplace decisions run on System 1.

Anchoring & Adjustment

The first number encountered pulls every subsequent estimate toward it.

Decoy / Compromise Effects

A deliberately inferior third option, or a safe middle option, can be added purely to steer choice toward a target item.

Availability Heuristic

Vivid, recent, or heavily covered events are judged as more probable than they actually are.

Choice Overload

Beyond roughly 8–15 options (AMO-dependent), more choice degrades rather than improves the decision experience.

Loss Aversion / Framing

Losses loom larger than equivalent gains; identical facts framed as a loss vs. a gain produce different decisions.

Mental Accounting

Money is treated as non-fungible across imaginary "buckets" defined by source or intended use.

Affect Heuristic

How an option feels substitutes for a full evaluation of its actual benefits and risks.

Every one of these heuristics and biases is a lever of choice architecture — the deliberate design of how options are presented (defaults, decoys, framing, pricing, choice-set size) to steer decisions without removing any option outright. Overview: What Is Self-Concept? ▾

Self-concept is a consumer's total subjective perception of themselves — their body, personality, capabilities, and so on. It is not a single, fixed picture: consumers hold several distinct self-images at once, and different brands and purchases are recruited to represent different versions of "you."

Multiple Selves ▾

Self-image splits along two crossed dimensions: who is doing the perceiving (self vs. others) and whether the image is realistic or aspirational (actual vs. ideal). This produces four distinct self-images that a person can hold simultaneously, and that different brands can be recruited to express.

Actual Self-Image

How you actually see yourself.

Ideal Self-Image

How you would like to see yourself.

Social Self-Image

How you think others see you.

Ideal Social Self-Image

How you would like others to see you.

Discussion Prompt

Who are "you"? Which brands represent each of these different "yous" — the person you actually are, the person you'd like to be, and the person you want your social circle to see?

Self-Esteem: How Marketers Reinforce (and Risk) It ▾

Self-esteem is a consumer's opinion about themselves — how positively they evaluate who they are. Marketers can position brands to reinforce self-worth and acceptance, though the same territory carries real reputational risk if execution feels opportunistic rather than genuine.

Reinforcing Self-Worth

Dove's Real Beauty campaign challenged narrow beauty standards to build self-acceptance — but also surfaced the spotlight effect (the tendency to overestimate how much others notice and judge our appearance).

Representation & Inclusion

Gillette's ad featuring a transgender boy extended self-esteem messaging into representation — signalling that a wider range of identities are seen and accepted by the brand.

Distorting Reality: Vanity Sizing ▾

Vanity sizing: labelling apparel with smaller sizes than the garment's actual measurements, so a "size 8" varies widely from one brand to the next. It is a direct play on self-concept — flattering the actual-vs-ideal self-image gap at the point of purchase.

Compensatory Consumption & Retail Therapy ▾

Compensatory consumption: purchase or consumption undertaken specifically to alleviate low self-esteem. It is driven by self-discrepancy — the perceived gap between one's actual and ideal self (Mandel et al., 2016) — and shows up everyday as retail therapy: shopping in order to feel better, rather than to fill a functional need.

Psychology of Luxury Consumption: Status Signaling ▾

Conspicuous consumption: the desire to provide prominent, visible evidence of one's ability to afford luxury goods — what Thorstein Veblen (1899) called a "wasteful exhibition." Louis Vuitton, Gucci, Hermès, and The Row occupy different points on this spectrum, from loud logo-driven signalling to deliberately understated, insider-only luxury.

Trickle-Down Effect

Lower classes adopt upper-class fashions and keep wearing them even after the upper class has abandoned them — precisely because they are no longer exclusive.

Trickle-Up Effect

To stay distinct, some people deliberately avoid conventional status symbols and adopt non-representative ones instead — uniqueness through non-conformity rather than acquisition.

Identity: Personal, Social, Gender & Cultural ▾

Personal identity: one's beliefs about the sort of person one is and how one differs from others (uniqueness) — the core, more enduring aspects of self that persist over time.

Social Identity Theory

People derive part of their self-concept from perceived membership in social groups (nationality, gender, profession, religion, even brands they like), intertwining personal identity with group identity. Lacking a positive personal identity, people often seek self-esteem through group identification instead — and when personal and social identities fuse (the boundary between self and group blurs), people become markedly more willing to fight or defend their group (Gómez et al., 2011; Swann et al., 2009).

Cult and cult-like brands (Apple, Royal Enfield, Harley-Davidson) succeed by supplying identity, not just product — consumers pay a real premium for the identity a brand lets them claim. Gender identity and cultural identity (holidays, rituals, and traditions) further shape which products and consumption patterns feel like "self-expression" versus a poor fit.

Caselet — Designing for Identity

If you were designing a pram (baby stroller), what features would you prioritize? The exercise pushes past pure functionality (safety, storage) into what the product signals about the parent's identity and values — a reminder that even utilitarian categories carry identity weight.

Key Concepts ▾
Multiple Selves

Actual, ideal, social, and ideal-social self-images can all be targeted by different brands or campaigns.

Compensatory Consumption

Purchases made to close a self-discrepancy gap and repair low self-esteem.

Conspicuous Consumption

Visible luxury spending as a signal of status and means.

Social Identity Theory

Self-concept is partly built from group memberships, including brand communities.

Overview: What Is Attitude? ▾

An attitude is a learned tendency to behave in a consistently favourable or unfavourable way toward a given object — how someone feels about it, and an antecedent of behaviour. The "attitude object" can be a product, brand, service, price, package, advertisement, promotional medium, or even the retailer selling the product. Attitudes are cognitions, not directly observable — marketers assess them by asking questions or inferring them from behaviour.

Tri-Component Attitude (ABC) Model ▾

Attitude is composed of three interlocking components (Schiffman et al.):

Affective (Emotional)

Feeling or emotional response. E.g. "I love the design of the iPhones."

Behavioural (Action)

Intention to act. E.g. "I intend to buy the new iPhone 17 when it becomes available."

Cognitive (Beliefs)

Knowledge or perception. E.g. "iPhones have a great camera, reliable performance, and strong resale value."

Example — Reusable Water Bottles

Cognitive: Alisha believes using reusable bottles helps reduce plastic waste and protect the environment. Affective: she feels proud and responsible when she uses her reusable bottle. Behavioural: she always carries it and avoids buying single-use plastic bottles.

The three components don't have to agree — a consumer can like a product but still not intend to buy it, which is exactly where the attitude–behaviour gap opens up.

The Attitude–Behaviour Gap ▾

Attitude ≠ behaviour, much like preference ≠ decision. Situational factors — affordability, feasibility, social pressure (peer pressure), availability — can disrupt the link between how a consumer feels and what they actually do.

Case — Starbucks' Reusable Cup Program

Starbucks positions itself around sustainability, discounting drinks served in personal tumblers and running reusable-cup lending programs. Consumer surveys consistently show customers believe reducing plastic/paper waste matters and express positive intentions to support eco-friendly initiatives. Yet only a small share of customers actually bring their own cup — most still take the disposable one. Favourable attitudes did not translate into sustainable behaviour, leaving Starbucks' marketing team to figure out how to close that gap.

The same gap shows up in the Ola Electric two-wheeler crisis — strong purchase enthusiasm and stated attitudes toward EVs didn't protect the brand once real-world service and reliability problems surfaced, exposing how fragile attitude-driven intent can be against situational friction.

Theory of Planned Behaviour ▾

The Theory of Planned Behaviour (Myers) explains intended behaviour as a function of three inputs: attitude toward the behaviour (do I think it's good or bad?), subjective norms (what do people important to me think?), and perceived behavioural control (how easy or difficult will this actually be for me?).

Example — Megha and Sustainable Fashion

Megha is deciding whether to adopt sustainable fashion (organic fabrics, ethical labor). Attitude: she believes sustainable brands reduce environmental harm and values ethics over trends. Subjective norm: influencers she follows promote sustainable fashion, and she cares about being seen as responsible in her circle. Perceived control: she has a stable income and can absorb the cost, and can manage shopping less frequently if needed. All three inputs point the same direction, making adoption likely.

Cognitive Dissonance ▾

Cognitive dissonance (Festinger, 1957): the psychological discomfort that arises from holding two or more inconsistent attitudes, behaviours, or thoughts. It is the theoretical basis for what happens when a consumer's multiple, competing attitudes collide.

Post-Purchase Dissonance ("Buyer's Remorse") ▾

Post-purchase dissonance is the psychological discomfort or second-guessing a consumer feels after making a purchase — attitude here follows behaviour, rather than the more usual attitude-precedes-behaviour sequence. It usually surfaces when the purchase was:

High Involvement

The decision was risky or personally important.

Choice Overload

Many alternatives were considered.

Irreversible

The consequences can't easily be undone.

Emotion-Driven

The purchase was impulsive rather than rationally evaluated.

Two common triggers worth flagging separately: a persistent salesperson can induce dissonance because the consumer feels "pushed" into the purchase, and conflicting information post-purchase (e.g. discovering a discount on the same product at a different store) can retroactively sour a decision that felt fine at the time.

Post-Purchase Rationalization ▾

Post-purchase rationalization, also called choice-supportive bias: the tendency to retroactively ascribe positive attributes to the option one selected and/or demote the options one passed on. It is, functionally, the opposite pull from post-purchase dissonance — a way of resolving the same underlying discomfort by talking oneself back into the choice rather than regretting it.

Marketers can reduce post-purchase dissonance directly: strong post-sale reassurance (confirmation emails, easy return policies, follow-up support), consistent pricing/promotion so customers don't discover a "better deal" elsewhere, and messaging that reinforces the specific benefits of the choice made.
Key Concepts ▾
ABC Model

Affective, behavioural, and cognitive components of attitude can diverge from one another.

Attitude–Behaviour Gap

Favourable attitudes don't reliably predict actual behaviour once situational friction enters.

Theory of Planned Behaviour

Attitude + subjective norms + perceived control jointly predict intended behaviour.

Post-Purchase Dissonance

Buyer's remorse, most likely after high-involvement, high-choice, irreversible, emotion-driven purchases.

Overview: What Is Culture? ▾

Culture is the collectively shared meanings, ways of life, and unifying values shared by members of a particular group — and it forms part of one's identity. Hofstede's Onion Model pictures culture as layers running from easy to observe and adopt (outer layers) to hard to observe and adopt (the inner, values core):

Hofstede's Onion Model — Outer to Inner

Symbols (most visible, easiest to copy) → Heroes → Rituals → Values (least visible, hardest to observe or adopt — the deep core of a culture).

Symbols, Heroes, Rituals & Values ▾
Symbols

Things that represent a culture. E.g. Vicks had to reformulate its marketing in Germany and Austria, and Pepsi's branding reads differently in China — the same symbol doesn't travel with identical meaning everywhere. The Taj Amritsar and Taj Bengal each lean on regional symbolism distinct from a generic "luxury hotel" identity.

Heroes

Individuals or groups respected within a culture. Local heroes endorsing Gillette Guard tapped culturally specific trust; Adidas partnering with Bad Bunny leaned on his stature within Latin culture. The Bud Light/Dylan Mulvaney controversy shows the flip side — a hero choice can badly misjudge a brand's existing cultural audience.

Rituals

Ceremonies and traditions important to a culture. Dunkin' Donuts failed to embed itself in Indian consumption rituals and shut down; Mad Over Donuts and Domino's, by contrast, adapted to local rituals and tastes and thrived.

Values

Principles and beliefs important to a culture — the hardest layer to observe or borrow. Nike's Pro Hijab signalled respect for a specific set of cultural/religious values rather than merely a product feature.

Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions ▾

Hofstede's cultural dimensions theory scores national cultures along axes such as power distance, individualism vs. collectivism, masculinity vs. femininity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term vs. short-term orientation, and indulgence vs. restraint. The long-term/short-term × indulgence/restraint quadrant illustrates how differently countries cluster:

Applied Exercise

Design a TV ad and tagline for life insurance for Mexico vs. Germany, and for a new skincare line for the USA vs. India — or design a Ready-to-Eat Meals brand (name, logo, ambassador, ritual/symbol) for markets scored on Hofstede's dimensions: Japan (high power distance, collectivist, masculine, high uncertainty avoidance, long-term, restrained), Sweden (low power distance, individualistic, feminine, low uncertainty avoidance, long-term, indulgent), Brazil (high power distance, collectivist, masculine, low uncertainty avoidance, short-term, indulgent).

How Products Acquire Cultural Meaning ▾

Products don't arrive with cultural meaning built in — they accumulate it through six recurring pathways:

Six Pathways to Cultural Meaning

1. Product Design — form itself signals meaning.
2. Communication — advertising builds association (Marlboro's cowboy imagery; Bata's ad campaigns).
3. Heroes — endorsers or associated figures transfer meaning (Ray-Ban Aviators, Zippo lighters, Nike Air Jordan).
4. Trends / Social Observers / Media — coverage and cultural commentary attach new meaning over time.
5. Rituals — repeated use patterns embed a product into cultural practice.
6. Creating a Sub-culture — a brand deliberately cultivates its own community with distinct norms and identity markers.

Key Concepts ▾
Onion Model

Symbols are easiest to observe/copy; values are the hardest, most enduring core.

Symbols, Heroes, Rituals, Values

Four layers a brand can tap into — but each carries different risk if misjudged.

Hofstede's Dimensions

National culture scores explain why identical campaigns land differently across markets.

Cultural Meaning Transfer

Design, communication, heroes, trends, rituals, and sub-culture creation are the six channels products use to acquire cultural meaning.

Principles of Persuasion

▾

Overview: What Is Persuasion? ▾

Persuasion is the act of influencing a person's beliefs, attitudes, motivations, or behaviour. Robert Cialdini's six principles of persuasion remain the field's most widely applied framework for how that influence actually operates in practice.

Cialdini's Six Principles of Persuasion ▾
1. Reciprocity

People feel obliged to return favours. Applications: free samples, free trials, test drives.

2. Consistency

People like to behave consistently with what they've previously said or done.

3. Social Proof

Especially under uncertainty, people look to others' actions to decide their own — "herd mentality." Applications: service reviews, testimonials, influencer endorsements.

4. Authority

People follow credible, knowledgeable experts. E.g. a stranger in uniform gets compliance a stranger in casual clothes wouldn't. Applications: certifications, years of experience, educational credentials.

5. Liking

People say yes more readily to people they like — those similar to them, who compliment them, who are physically attractive (halo effect), or who cooperate toward shared goals. Applications: charming sales staff, celebrity endorsements, leaders chosen for shared values.

6. Scarcity

People want more of what they can have less of. Worth interrogating in practice — are diamonds actually as scarce as their marketing implies?

Consistency in Practice: Foot-in-the-Door & Door-in-the-Face ▾
Foot-in-the-Door Technique

Getting agreement to a small favor first makes people more likely to say yes to a larger favor later. E.g. a donation ask that starts at "any amount" or "sponsor one meal for a month," or a salesperson who first asks only for "two minutes."

Door-in-the-Face Technique

The reverse sequence: open with a larger request expected to be rejected, then follow with the actually-wanted smaller request, which now looks reasonable by comparison. It works partly by leaning on guilt after the initial refusal.

Narrative Persuasion — Storytelling ▾

Persuasion through story rather than argument — narrative lowers a listener's counter-arguing (they're absorbed in the story, not scrutinizing claims) and builds emotional identification with characters, making the underlying message land with less resistance than a direct claim would.

Elaboration Likelihood Model ▾

The Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM) explains that persuasion can travel through two different routes, depending on how much a person elaborates on (thinks through) the message:

Central Route Processing

High elaboration — the audience carefully evaluates the actual arguments and evidence. Persuasion here is durable and resistant to counter-argument.

Peripheral Route Processing

Low elaboration — the audience relies on surface cues (attractiveness of the source, production quality, emotional tone) rather than substance. Persuasion here is faster to achieve but more easily reversed.

What Drives Elaboration?

Motivation (involvement in the topic) × Ability (knowledge, cognitive capacity, need for cognition) × Opportunity (time available, environment, message characteristics) jointly determine whether a message gets processed centrally or peripherally.

Key Concepts ▾
Cialdini's Six Principles

Reciprocity, consistency, social proof, authority, liking, and scarcity — the recurring levers of everyday persuasion.

Foot-in-the-Door / Door-in-the-Face

Sequencing a request's size — small-then-large, or large-then-small — changes compliance odds.

ELM: Central vs. Peripheral

High-elaboration persuasion is durable; low-elaboration persuasion via surface cues is faster but more fragile.

Embodied Cognition and Consumer Wellbeing

Overview: Embodied Cognition ▾

Embodied cognition holds that bodily sensations and physical experience shape abstract judgement — consumers don't just think with their brains in the abstract; physical states (posture, temperature, touch) get metaphorically mapped onto unrelated evaluations like power, trust, or product quality.

Verticality & Motor Fluency ▾

The motor fluency effect (Elder & Krishna, 2012, on the "visual depiction effect" in advertising) shows that depicting a product in an orientation that facilitates embodied mental simulation of using it changes how favourably it's evaluated.

Verticality — Higher Spatial Positioning

Placing a person or object higher in physical space is metaphorically linked to: higher perceived power/status; increased risk-seeking; and higher-level construal — i.e. choices favouring long-term benefits over short-term convenience.

Haptic Temperature & the Temperature Premium Effect ▾

Physical warmth maps metaphorically onto interpersonal warmth: holding something warm promotes social warmth, fosters interpersonal trust, enhances positive assessment of others, and increases purchase intentions.

Temperature Premium Effect

Zwebner, Lee & Goldenberg (2014) found that warm ambient temperatures increase product valuation — consumers are willing to pay more for the same product when they are physically warmer, independent of the product itself.

Colour: Warmth & Perception ▾
Warm Colours as Cues

Chandler & Schwarz (2010): treating objects as "alive" (anthropomorphizing them) makes people less willing to replace them. Guéguen & Jacob (2014): a coffee cup's colour measurably changes how "warm" the beverage inside is perceived to be.

Colour and Trait Inference

Labrecque & Milne (2012): red reads as exciting, blue reads as competent — colour choice in branding and marketing communicates trait-level meaning well beyond simple aesthetics.

Contagion Effects ▾
Consumer Contagion Effect

Products touched or tried by other, ordinary consumers get evaluated less favourably (Argo, Dahl & Morales, 2006) — physical contact "contaminates" perceived value.

Positive Consumer Contagion

Purchase likelihood goes up when a product was touched by an attractive confederate of the opposite gender, or owned/touched by a celebrity (Argo, Dahl & Morales, 2008; Newman, Diesendruck & Bloom, 2011) — contagion cuts both ways depending on who did the touching.

Product Contagion Effect

Disgusting products are believed to transfer offensive properties to other products they merely touch, negatively skewing evaluations of the second, unrelated product (Morales & Fitzsimons, 2007).

Consumer Wellbeing & the Need Satisfaction Model ▾

Under one common conceptualization, Quality of Life (QOL) rests on humanistic need satisfaction: the more a product satisfies a consumer's underlying needs, the more it enhances their sense of wellbeing.

Frugal innovation ("jugaad") is a direct route to consumer wellbeing through accessible, low-cost innovation rather than premium features: Savlon's low-cost antiseptic innovation, Mitti Cool's clay refrigerator, and Clocky — an alarm clock that physically rolls away, forcing its owner out of bed to catch and silence it — all solve real functional problems through design rather than expense.

Societal Wellbeing: CSR, Cause Marketing & Social Marketing ▾

The Societal Marketing Concept requires marketers to meet target-audience needs in ways that improve, preserve, and enhance society's wellbeing while still meeting business objectives.

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

A business model integrating social and environmental concerns into operations and stakeholder relationships instead of considering only economic profit — commonly framed as a triple bottom line (people, planet, profit).

Cause Marketing

A collaborative effort between a for-profit firm and a non-profit/cause, for mutual benefit — the firm gains patronage from consumers who want to support the cause; the non-profit gets funding. Also called cause-related marketing (e.g. Nihar Shanti Amla's cause-linked campaigns; Amazon's "Box to Beds" repurposing program).

Social Marketing

"Marketing for good": using marketing knowledge, concepts, and techniques to influence behaviour that benefits individuals and communities, usually run by government or non-profit organizations rather than for-profit firms.

Nudges & Choice Architecture ▾

Choice architecture is the deliberate crafting of a choice environment. A nudge is any aspect of that choice architecture that alters behaviour in a predictable way without forbidding any option or materially changing economic incentives — the underlying philosophy is libertarian paternalism: legitimate ways to influence behaviour while still respecting freedom of choice.

Case — West Japan Railway's Drunk-Fall Deaths

In 2013, West Japan Railway faced 221 incidents of intoxicated people falling onto tracks. Reviewing security footage rather than trusting intuition, the company found that in 90% of cases, people stood up from a station bench and walked straight forward off the platform edge, as if sleepwalking. The fix: rotate the benches 90 degrees, so standing up no longer pointed people at the tracks — no rule change, no signage, just a redesigned default path.

Case — Chicago's Lake Shore Drive Curve

Chicago's most dangerous curve kept producing crashes despite fines and posted speed limits. The city instead painted transverse lines on the road that appear to compress as the curve tightens, visually cueing drivers that they're accelerating — a purely perceptual nudge that produced a 36% decrease in crashes over the next six months.

Processing fluency nudges show up in retail layout too: healthy foods are preferred more often when positioned to the left of unhealthy foods rather than the right (Romero & Biswas, 2016) — a small, low-cost default with a measurable effect on healthier choice.

Social Entrepreneurship & Brand Activism ▾

Social entrepreneurship: enterprises (for-profit or not-for-profit) focused on societal wellbeing and solving societal/environmental problems — e.g. The Big Issue's "Change Please Coffee," which employs people experiencing homelessness as baristas.

Brand activism: when a brand takes a public stand on a social, environmental, economic, or political issue — supporting a polarizing cause aligned with the company's core values, and committing to visible action, not just messaging. It carries real reputational risk when execution or timing misreads the audience.

Brand activism cuts both ways: Nike's support for Colin Kaepernick and Benetton's AIDS-awareness campaign are held up as landmark, values-driven activism, while Tanishq's #BoycottTanishq controversy (2020) shows how even a well-intentioned harmony-themed campaign can spark backlash disproportionate to the brand's actual message — activism only works when the audience reads the brand's stated values as authentic.
Key Concepts ▾
Embodied Cognition

Physical sensation (height, warmth, touch) metaphorically shapes abstract judgement about products and people.

Contagion Effects

Who or what touched a product before you changes how you evaluate it — positively or negatively.

Need Satisfaction Model

Products enhance quality of life to the extent they genuinely satisfy underlying needs.

Nudges

Choice-architecture changes that redirect behaviour without removing options or changing incentives.

Brand Activism

Public stands on social issues — high potential upside, high potential backlash if perceived as inauthentic.
